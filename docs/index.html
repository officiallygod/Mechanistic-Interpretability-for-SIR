Research Paradigm

Core Methodological Pillars

01

The Superposition Problem

Standard ResNet backbones possess only 512 channels at layer4 before pooling. When encoding rich manipulation environments, individual neurons exhibit polysemantic superposition: a single channel fires for multiple unrelated features.

02

Unsupervised Sparse Dictionary

We tap spatial activations before pooling and train an overcomplete Top-K Sparse Autoencoder (M=4096, K=32). Training is completely offline and unsupervised, reconstructing vectors without reward or simulator feedback.

03

Causal Closed-Loop Proof

Active features are projected to heatmaps and matched with simulator object masks to form a latent kept set. Closed-loop rollouts under feature ablation verify Discovery, Removal (invariance), and Consequence.

Theoretical Foundations

Architectural Principles & Hypotheses

Foundations of polysemanticity, dictionary learning, spatial resolution, and causal evaluation.

01 Superposition

Why is an SAE required beyond the standard ResNet representations?

The pre-pooling ResNet representation has 512 channels at `layer4`. Manipulation environments contain hundreds of distinct visual properties. Due to limited channel capacity, individual neurons exhibit polysemanticity: a single channel activates for multiple unrelated visual attributes. The SAE projects these activations into an overcomplete 4,096-dimensional basis where individual directions isolate single semantic factors.

02 Unsupervised

How is the SAE trained, and does training depend on task performance?

The SAE is trained strictly offline on recorded activation vectors without environment rollouts or reward feedback. The optimization objective is purely unsupervised: minimizing reconstruction error under a Top-K sparsity constraint: \(\min \|\mathbf{x} - \hat{\mathbf{x}}\|^2\) subject to \(\|f\|_0 \le K\).

03 Sparsity Pareto

What prevents an identity shortcut, and why select K=32?

Because 4,064 of the 4,096 dictionary coordinates are zeroed out on every forward pass, an identity shortcut cannot form. Setting \(K=32\) (~6% active capacity) provides an empirical balance: capturing over 95% of activation variance while preserving isolated, monosemantic feature directions.

04 Separation

Does the language instruction condition the SAE representation?

No. The visual backbone processes camera frames independently, while the CLIP text embedding is supplied to the downstream MDT action generator. The SAE intercepts visual activations prior to multimodal fusion, isolating visual perception from task goal conditioning.

05 Affordances

Does activation on localized components invalidate object discovery?

No. Complex objects are represented compositionally in deep vision networks (for example, a microwave decomposed into door, handle, and interface). When an active feature localizes specifically to an interactive component like a handle rather than the entire object silhouette, it indicates that the representation isolates functional affordances relevant to manipulation.

06 Resolution

How do input resolution differences affect the spatial feature map?

In `SIR_Model`, images are natively stored at \(128 \times 128\), resulting in a \(4 \times 4\) spatial grid (16 tokens) at `layer4`. In standard ImageNet models with \(224 \times 224\) inputs, `layer4` yields a \(7 \times 7\) grid (49 tokens). The architecture supports both the native \(4 \times 4\) stream and an upscaled \(224 \times 224\) stream for finer spatial localization.

07 Controls

What role do distractors play in causal verification?

RoboCasa kitchen scenes contain task-irrelevant objects besides the target (exact counts vary by task and scene; not yet measured). These distractors serve as negative controls: ablating the features of a task-critical part such as the handle should hurt success much more than ablating equally active distractor features. Expected: Success(handle ablated) << Success(distractor ablated).

08 Leakage

What motivated this approach over explicit graph selection methods like SIR?

Prior work (ICLR 2027) showed that SIR suffered from information leakage: excluded objects can still influence the action through the removal mechanism (e.g. softmax denominators over all nodes, survivor score weighting, presence counts). Leaky SIR fails Discovery and Removal; the corrected version passes Removal and Consequence but drops from 2.12 to 1.62 average length on CALVIN (Fig. 1). No design point passes all three conditions. Unsupervised latent discovery instead analyzes internal representations of an untouched end-to-end policy.

Empirical Verification

The 3 Foundational Verification Conditions

Adopted from the In-Loop Subset Explanations (ILSE) framework to benchmark our Latent Kept Set.

D-C 1. DISCOVERY

Discovery Condition

The object selection must emerge naturally from the policy's own representations, rather than being supplied by an external simulator oracle or ground-truth bounding box list.

✓ Passes by construction: Raw pixel policy has zero object supervision.
R-C 2. REMOVAL

Removal Condition

Excluded objects must have zero causal influence on the generated trajectory chunk. Clamping non-selected object features to zero in the SAE must leave the motor action unchanged.

Requirement: Δa = ||a_original - a_ablated|| ≈ 0
C-C 3. CONSEQUENCE

Consequence Condition

Ablating the latent kept set features must cause task failure, while ablating a matched random set of distractor features must preserve policy completion.

Selectivity: Success(Kept Ablated) ≪ Success(Distractor)

1 Cameras: 2 static vs. all 3 Start with left + right: matches the published image baseline. Add the wrist camera later for part-level features; it needs a retrained policy and its view changes heavily during a rollout. 9 / 6
2 ResNet: single-task fine-tune vs. none The baseline already trains the ResNets end to end (on all 24 tasks). Train single-task on CloseDrawer (71 % baseline) for a clear success margin. A frozen ImageNet ResNet only as a control, since a weak policy makes removal effects uninformative. 8 / 4
3 Resolution: 128 vs. 224 Stay at 128. The demos are stored at 128, so 224 means re-rendering the dataset and losing comparability with SIR. Get finer resolution from layer 3 instead. 9 / 3
4 Tap: layer 3 vs. layer 4 Record both in one pass. Layer 3 (8 × 8 × 256) should hold parts, layer 4 (4 × 4 × 512) whole objects; comparing them answers the whole-object vs. part question. 9
5 Stack cameras vs. one at a time One SAE per camera on the spatial map before pooling (the 1 × 1 × 512 vector has no location left). Stacking feature maps cannot work: each camera has its own ResNet, and the randomized camera poses leave no fixed correspondence between cells. The MDT already fuses cameras as separate tokens. 9 / 2
6 Feed SAE output to the MDT Required. Splicing the reconstruction into the rollout is what enables Discovery, Removal and Consequence; without it we only have correlations. Check spliced success against the original first. 10
7 Compare kept set with SIR Yes. Ground SAE features to objects via the dataset's bounding boxes and compare with SIR's top-k set. SIR is a different (graph) policy, so agreement means both rely on the same objects, not the same mechanism. 8
8 FiLM ResNet vs. plain ResNet Optional extension. The code supports a language-conditioned ResNet (use_film), which makes visual features goal-dependent. Needs its own trained policy, and it matters most in multi-task or door tasks where the instruction varies. 4

The gap no paper covers yet
SIRILSE

Our gap: robot-policy SAE papers (P1, P2, P5, P7) use transformer VLAs with token or pooled vectors and ground features to motion or state concepts. Vision SAE papers (P8, P9) are spatial but study classifiers. Nobody combines spatial CNN SAEs inside a robot policy with object-level grounding and closed-loop D-R-C.

Base papers (lab-internal, not embedded)

Foundation
  • SIR (Mattes et al., CVPR 2026): scene-graph policy that learns an explicit, sparse kept set of objects. Our comparison target.
  • ILSE (under review, ICLR 2027): defines the Discovery, Removal and Consequence conditions we test on an implicit, SAE-based object set.

Report outline

  2. 2 · Foundations.

    • 2.2 Sparse autoencoders: L1 vs. TopK, dead latents, AuxK (P3).
    • 2.3 Causal evaluation: ablation types, the error term, faithfulness and completeness (P4).
    • 2.4 What makes a subset explanation valid: Discovery, Removal, Consequence (ILSE).
  3. 3 · Explicit object-set explanations. SIR: scene graphs, learned pruning, what the kept set revealed (dataset biases, static fixtures), and where ILSE shows explicit bottlenecks leak.
  4. 4 · Interpreting visual representations.
    • 4.1 Grounding units to concepts with segmentation IoU (P6).
    • 4.2 Spatial SAEs on vision models: per-patch features and their spatial maps (P9), CNN feature maps (P8).
    • 4.3 Lesson: single features rarely decide an output; concepts are distributed.
  5. 5 · Sparse autoencoders in robot policies.
    • 5.1 General vs. memorized features and steering (P5).
    • 5.2 Concepts vs. motor programs across six architectures (P2).
    • 5.3 Grounding features in behavioral events (P1).
    • 5.4 Named features for failure detection (P7).
    • 5.5 Comparison table: model, tap point, SAE, grounding, causal test, closed loop.
  6. 6 · Discussion. Cross-cutting findings: good reconstruction does not mean preserved behavior (P1, P2, P9); pooled vs. spatial activations; grounding to motions or states vs. to objects; how far SAE studies satisfy Discovery, Removal and Consequence. Open problem: implicit object sets in visuomotor policies.
  7. 7 · Conclusion. Summary and research directions.
